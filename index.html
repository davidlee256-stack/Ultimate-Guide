01

You can't evaluate forty tools and run projects at the same time.

Every week something new promises to redraw your plans, write your reports, or pull a scheme out of a sketch. A few of them are genuinely useful. Working out which is a research job nobody is paying you for.

  1. i

    The demos aren't project work.

    Launch videos run on clean, invented briefs. Your model carries four consultants' revisions and a client who rewrote the brief in month seven. Tools behave differently there, and the difference is the whole story.

  2. ii

    Nobody publishes the failures.

    Vendors show you the output that worked. The useful information is what the tool produced on the third attempt, and whether correcting it took longer than doing the job by hand.

  3. iii

    The cost of finding out lands on you.

    A trial account, an afternoon of setup, a week of fighting the output, then the quiet decision to go back to the old way. That is the real price of evaluating a tool — and you pay it per tool.

This guide is that evaluation work, already done and written down in the order you'd need it.

02

What's inside

Ten parts, 62 chapters. The book runs from deciding whether a tool deserves your afternoon, through the project stages where AI currently earns its place, to the points where it reliably breaks.

  1. Part 01 Where AI actually fits in architectural practice 6 chapters

    A map of the work architecture teams do and the handful of places where current tools change the effort involved. Read this first so the rest of the book has somewhere to land.

  2. Part 02 Choosing tools: a test you can run in an afternoon 7 chapters

    A repeatable way to judge a new tool quickly: what to feed it, what to measure, and when to stop. Built so you can apply it to whatever launches after this book does.

  3. Part 03 Concept work and early-stage image generation 6 chapters

    Where generated imagery helps at concept stage, how to keep it tied to a real site and brief, and how to stop it quietly setting the design direction for you.

  4. Part 04 Drawing, modelling and the BIM question 7 chapters

    What current tools do and don't do inside a modelling workflow, where the integrations are real versus announced, and what happens to your file when they touch it.

  5. Part 05 Writing: reports, specifications and client correspondence 6 chapters

    The area where these tools are strongest and the checking discipline that makes that safe — particularly for anything that leaves the office with your name on it.

  6. Part 06 Research, precedent and reading regulation 7 chapters

    Using AI to get through dense documents faster, and the verification step that has to follow. Includes what these tools get confidently wrong about regulation.

  7. Part 07 Visualisation and presentation 6 chapters

    Turning models and sketches into presentable images, keeping them accurate enough to show a client, and being straight about what has been generated.

  8. Part 08 Practice admin, bids and fee proposals 6 chapters

    The unglamorous work — submissions, proposals, meeting notes, chasing information — where the time actually goes and where automation is least risky.

  9. Part 09 Where the tools fall apart 6 chapters

    The failure modes, collected: confident wrong answers, output that looks finished and isn't, and the tasks that consistently cost more time than they save.

  10. Part 10 Client data, disclosure and professional risk 5 chapters

    What you're agreeing to when you upload a client's material, what to tell clients, and how to set a practice policy people will actually follow. Practical, not legal advice.

Not written for

This is written for people choosing tools, not for people making them. You'd be reading it sideways.
You already run structured tool evaluations
If you keep your own notes on everything you've tested, you're probably ahead of this book already.
You need legal or contractual advice
Part 10 covers client data and risk in practical terms. It isn't professional advice and doesn't pretend to be.
