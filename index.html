01

You can't evaluate forty tools and run projects at the same time.

Every week something new promises to redraw your plans, write your reports, or pull a scheme out of a sketch. A few of them are genuinely useful. Working out which is a research job nobody is paying you for.

  1. i

    The demos aren't project work.

    Launch videos run on clean, invented briefs. Your model carries four consultants' revisions and a client who rewrote the brief in month seven. Tools behave differently there, and the difference is the whole story.

  2. ii

    Nobody publishes the failures.

    Vendors show you the output that worked. The useful information is what the tool produced on the third attempt, and whether correcting it took longer than doing the job by hand.

  3. iii

    The cost of finding out lands on you.

    A trial account, an afternoon of setup, a week of fighting the output, then the quiet decision to go back to the old way. That is the real price of evaluating a tool — and you pay it per tool.

This guide is that evaluation work, already done and written down in the order you'd need it.

03

Who this is written for — and who should skip it

Written for

Practice owners and directors
You decide what the practice adopts, what it pays for, and what it tells clients about how the work gets made.
Project architects and job runners
You need tools that hold up on a live job with a real programme, not on a demo file.
Part 2s and recent graduates
You'll be the person asked to "look into AI". This gives you something defensible to come back with.
BIM coordinators and technologists
The modelling, data and file-hygiene chapters are written with your side of the job in mind.
Students heading into practice
Learn where these tools sit in a real workflow before someone hands you one and expects an answer.

Not written for

You want a pack of prompts to paste in
Prompts go stale in weeks. This is about which tools to use and where, which changes far more slowly.
You want software that designs the building
It doesn't exist. The book is blunt about that, repeatedly, and you may find that annoying.
You're building AI products for the industry
This is written for people choosing tools, not for people making them. You'd be reading it sideways.
You already run structured tool evaluations
If you keep your own notes on everything you've tested, you're probably ahead of this book already.
You need legal or contractual advice
Part 10 covers client data and risk in practical terms. It isn't professional advice and doesn't pretend to be.

Who wrote this

I run AI for Architects, a YouTube channel about the practical use of AI tools in architectural work. I test software against the kinds of tasks architecture teams do, and I publish what happens — including the runs where the tool loses.

My side of this is the testing. I put each tool through the same work repeatedly, record where it holds and where it gives way, and write it up in terms the people who do run projects can act on. Practice is their expertise. How the software actually behaves is what I can tell you about.

That shapes the book. It's organised around workflows and evidence rather than opinions about design, and where a tool's value depends on how a particular practice works, it says so instead of guessing.
